# LinkedIn posts

Load this profile for LinkedIn posts about scientific research, research software, publications, technical projects, or related professional work.

Apply the core `write-like-a-scientist` rules first.

## Write as the person who did the work

Use first person when describing the author's own work. Write as a researcher or engineer reporting what was done, not as a marketer announcing a product.

Start with the project, result, method, or reason for the work. Do not open with generic enthusiasm.

## Give only the history that matters

Include development history when it explains why the work exists, where it came from, or what changed. Keep it factual and short.

If work was completed quickly, distinguish new work from methods, code, data, or research that already existed. Do not imply that established work was created from scratch during the stated period.

Do not manufacture a personal story merely to make a post more engaging.

## Keep technical detail concrete

Name the scientific operations, datasets, methods, measurements, or implementation choices that make the work useful or interesting.

Use a short list when it lets a technical reader understand the scope quickly. Do not turn the post into documentation or an API inventory.

For performance, accuracy, parity, or validation claims, state enough of the comparison or test to make the claim meaningful. Prefer a measured result to an adjective.

## Preserve scientific uncertainty

State unfinished validation, known limitations, or remaining scientific review when they materially affect the claim.

Do not turn passing tests, parity checks, automated review, or model output into a claim of scientific validation.

Separate what has been measured or checked from what is planned, inferred, or still under review.

## Describe AI by its actual role

Mention AI models when their contribution is material to the work or to the point of the post. Name the tasks they performed when useful, such as analysis, implementation, reconstruction, review, or validation assistance.

Do not describe work only as `AI-powered`, and do not make AI the headline when the scientific or technical work is the subject.

Human review and model-assisted review should remain distinct when that distinction matters to the claim.

## Prefer restrained formatting

LinkedIn posts should remain readable as plain text.

- Use short paragraphs.
- Use bullets when they make technical scope easier to scan.
- Do not use fake Unicode bold or italic text.
- Do not add hashtags by default.
- Mention people or organizations only when they have a real connection to the work.
- Avoid decorative emoji unless the author's established style calls for one.

A descriptive title can help a substantial project or publication post, but do not force every post to have one.

## Keep links focused

Give the reader one obvious primary destination when possible.

When several links are relevant, keep the most useful one in the post and move secondary links to a comment when that makes the post easier to read. Do not make this a fixed rule; use the destination that best serves the post.

Do not make unsupported claims about platform ranking or external-link penalties.

Keep calls to action factual, for example `Documentation:`, `Read online:`, or `Source:`. Avoid manufactured engagement prompts unless there is a genuine question for the audience.

## Use images for information

Use an image when it communicates scientific or technical information that is difficult to get from the text alone.

Prefer a scientific visualization, a publication figure or cover, a readable benchmark graphic, or an actual interface when it demonstrates relevant behavior.

One strong image is usually preferable to several redundant screenshots. Do not attach a generic logo, terminal screenshot, or link-preview image merely because an image slot is available.

## Keep professional context separate when needed

For independent research, open-source work, or outside projects, make the relationship to the author's main employment clear when ambiguity would matter. Do not add disclaimers or employer references when there is no realistic ambiguity.

## Final pass

Before returning a LinkedIn post, check:

- Does it sound like a researcher or engineer describing their own work?
- Is the main scientific or technical point apparent near the beginning?
- Is relevant project history accurate rather than dramatized?
- Are numerical and validation claims supported by the stated evidence?
- Are unfinished scientific checks stated when they matter?
- Is AI described by the work it actually performed?
- Can any marketing phrase, generic enthusiasm, hashtag, or unnecessary mention be removed?
- Is there one clear primary destination when the post needs a link?
- Does an attached image add information rather than decoration?
